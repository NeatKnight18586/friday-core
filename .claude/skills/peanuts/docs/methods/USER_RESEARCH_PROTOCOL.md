# User and Field Research Protocol

**Status:** B5 method specification  
**Version:** 0.1.0-draft  
**Applies to:** Discovery interviews, observation, contextual inquiry, diary studies, usability tests, surveys and field pilots

## 1. Purpose

Gather evidence about real people, their contexts, needs, behaviors, workarounds, constraints and experiences. Use the method that best answers the decision question. User research is not a sales pitch and must not be used to manufacture support for a favored idea.

## 2. Plan before recruiting

Specify objective, decision, research questions, method, target groups, inclusion/exclusion criteria, recruitment channels, sample rationale, likely bias, accessibility/language needs, consent, data handling, and analysis plan. Research rounds should answer prioritized questions that the team can act on. Revisit the plan when findings change the question.

## 3. Participant selection

Recruit for relevant experience, not convenience alone. Consider different roles, experience levels, contexts, accessibility needs, and people who may be excluded by a digital-first assumption. Disclose convenience sampling and missing segments. No universal sample size applies across interviews, usability testing, surveys and experiments.

GOV.UK's service-research guidance gives 4–8 participants as a usual range for some qualitative rounds and notes that surveys/experiments used for clear population estimates need much larger samples. Treat this as a contextual reference, not a universal sample-size rule: https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/plan-user-research-for-your-service

## 4. Session method

Prefer recent, concrete past events and actual artifacts. Ask neutral prompts such as:

- Tell me about the last time this happened.
- What were you trying to accomplish?
- What did you do first, and what happened next?
- What workarounds or tools did you use?
- Where did the process become difficult, costly, slow, risky or inaccessible?
- What happened when the normal approach failed?

Avoid leading questions, solution-pitching, and asking only whether someone would use a hypothetical product. Separate verbatim quotations from paraphrases and researcher interpretation.

## 5. Consent, safeguarding and privacy

Before participation, explain who is conducting the research, purpose, activities, data collected, observation/recording, use/sharing, retention/deletion, voluntary participation, withdrawal and contact route. Obtain and preserve appropriate consent before participation and recording. Collect only necessary data.

Use pseudonymous participant references in project files. A research-round record may list `participant_refs` so findings can be checked against the round; this is not an identity register. Do not store names, contact details, consent forms, identifiable raw recordings, or sensitive participant information in Git or ordinary project JSONL records. Place approved raw material in a separately controlled location with access, retention and deletion rules.

If participants are minors or vulnerable, the topic is sensitive, personal/special-category data is involved, or institutional/legal/ethics approval may apply, pause and obtain appropriate local expert review and approvals. GOV.UK guidance is an operational reference; it is not a substitute for applicable Philippine law, institutional policy, or ethics-board advice.

## 6. Analysis

Separate observation, participant statement, interpretation and recommendation. Code or group patterns, compare segments, preserve dissent, and link each synthesized finding to source/claim/question/round IDs. AI may assist with de-identified note organization, but a human researcher must inspect the synthesis and retain responsibility for interpretation.

Do not infer population prevalence from a small convenience sample. Do not represent hypothetical personas or synthetic test users as real participants. Do not claim saturation merely because interviews repeat; state the scope and sampling limitations.

## 7. Output

Use the User Research Plan, Interview Guide, Research Synthesis and Opportunity Map templates as appropriate. Report who was and was not represented, how participants were recruited, what evidence was collected, key patterns and contrary cases, limitations, decision changes, and next tests.
